Two ways to count

Korean has two complete sets of numbers, learned side by side and used in strict division of labour. Which one you reach for depends on what you are counting; and telling the time forces you to use both at once. Four short modules; the numbers do the teaching.

Module 01

Two sets of numbers

Myth we'll unpick: "learning to count in Korean is learning one list"

It is two lists. One is native Korean, grown from the language itself; the other is Sino-Korean, built from Chinese roots. A learner has to hold both.

One language, two counting words for everything

Ask a Korean speaker for the number three and you can get 셋 (set) or 삼 (sam), and both are right; they just belong to different jobs. The first is a native Korean word, as old as the language. The second is Sino-Korean, borrowed from Chinese more than a thousand years ago, the way English borrowed its Latin numerals for the calendar and the clock.

Japanese has the same double system, for the same reason. English has a faint version of it too: we count one, two, three, but we say January is the first month, not the "oneth".

One to ten, both ways

Sino-Korean

The native set thins out fast; in everyday use it is rarely pushed past ninety-nine. The Sino set carries zero, the hundreds, and everything large. That division is Module 02.

How high each set really goes

Native Korean

Has its own words for the tens; 열 (10), 스물 (20), 서른 (30), on to 아흔 (90). It has no everyday word for zero, and its old words for a hundred and a thousand have fallen out of use, so in practice it stops around ninety-nine.

Sino-Korean

Runs without limit: 백 (100), 천 (1,000), 만 (10,000), 억 (100 million). It also has zero, 영 or 공. Large numbers, money and maths are all its work.

Korean groups large numbers in ten-thousands rather than thousands, so a million is 백만 (baengman), literally "a hundred ten-thousands".

Module 02

Which system, when

The choice is not free; each situation demands one set or the other. Learn the map of which goes where, and the rest is vocabulary.

Pick a situation

The clock makes you use both

The hour is native, the minutes are Sino-Korean, so a single time runs the two systems together in one breath. Half past three is 세 시 삼십 분, se si samsip bun: 세 (native three) for the hour, 삼십 (Sino thirty) for the minutes. Get the halves the wrong way round and you sound, to a Korean ear, like someone reading a clock in a language they half-know.

The counter for the hour is 시 (si); for minutes it is 분 (bun). The same syllable 분 is also the polite counter for people, one of many counters Module 03 introduces.

Module 03

Numbers that change shape

You rarely count in the bare numbers. You count with a counter word, and a handful of the native numbers quietly change form when a counter follows.

The counter word

Korean does not say "three dogs"; it says dogs, three head-of-animal: 개 세 마리, gae se mari. The counter names what kind of thing is being counted, the way English says two head of cattle or three sheets of paper, but Korean does it for everything. Different things take different counters: 개 for objects, 명 for people, 마리 for animals, 살 for years of age, 시 for the hour.

Watch the number change before the counter

Now you choose

Four apples. Objects take the native numbers and the counter 개; and 넷 (four) changes shape before a counter. So it is:

네 개 ne gae넷 shortens to 네. The pattern: apples first, then the number and counter; 사과 네 개, sagwa ne gae.

Twelve o'clock. The hour is native, with the counter 시; and 열둘 (twelve) changes before a counter. So it is:

열두 시 yeoldu si열둘 becomes 열두. The minutes, if you added them, would switch to Sino-Korean.

A coffee costs 4,500 won. Money is Sino-Korean. So it is:

사천오백 원 sacheon-obaek wonNo shape change at all; the Sino-Korean numbers never shorten before a counter. Only the native set does that, which is half of why the two are worth keeping straight.
Module 04

Where the two sets came from

Two number systems in one language is not clutter; it is a fossil record of contact, and Korean is not alone in carrying it.

Why a language keeps two

The native setThe native Korean numbers are inherited, part of the language's own oldest stock, and they attach to the counter words the culture built up for tallying concrete things; people, animals, objects, ages.This is the set that changes shape before a counter, a sign of how deeply worn-in it is.
The Sino setThe Sino-Korean numbers came in with centuries of Chinese literary and administrative influence, alongside the Hanja characters, and took over the work of writing; dates, money, measurement, mathematics, anything large or precise.Roughly half of the Korean vocabulary is Sino-Korean in origin; the numbers are only its most everyday face.
The parallelJapanese carries the very same split, native versus Sino-Japanese, for the same historical reason; sustained borrowing from Chinese laid a second numeral system over an older one.Where the two languages differ is in exactly which situations take which set.
The divideThe two Koreas have drifted in their vocabulary since 1945, and specialist and borrowed words have drifted most; but the two number systems and the division of labour between them are shared north and south.Some things are older and deeper than the politics laid over them.

Why this matters

Once you stop seeing two arbitrary lists and start seeing one native set for handling things and one borrowed set for measuring the world, the choice stops being memorisation and becomes a question you can answer: am I counting, or am I measuring? Korean asks it of you every time you tell the time.
