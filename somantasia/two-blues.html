Two blues — голубой and синий

A Russian speaker looking at a summer sky and a bottle of ink is not using two shades of one word. The sky is голубой and the ink is синий, and these are not "light blue" and "dark blue" in the English manner, a modifier hung on a shared noun; they are two separate basic colour terms, as separate as English green and blue. Russian is the standard textbook exception to the claim that no language needs more than eleven.

The eleven-term ceiling comes from Brent Berlin and Paul Kay's 1969 survey of basic colour terms; a basic term being, roughly, a word every native speaker knows, that names a colour and nothing else, and that is not a kind of some other colour the way scarlet is a kind of red. Ian Davies and Greville Corbett put Russian through the standard tests in 1994, eliciting colour lists from speakers and having them name a fixed set of tiles, and concluded that both синий and голубой qualify. Twelve basic terms, two of them in blue. Galina Paramei, the scholar who has worked longest on the problem, put the argument of her 2005 paper in its title: these are culturally basic terms; the lexicon was shaped by Russian history rather than by any peculiarity of Russian eyes.

The history is visible in the words themselves. Голубой is transparently built on голубь, the dove, from the blue-grey shimmer of a pigeon's neck; for centuries it lived close to grey, and survived into the eighteenth century as a horse-coat colour. Синий is old Common Slavic, with cousins across the Slavic languages, and its deeper etymology is unresolved; the dictionaries offer both a "shining" root and a "dark, grey" one, and no one can choose between them. In early Russian texts синий keeps dark company, collocating with soot and storm water, its prestige carried by expensive indigo-dyed cloth; голубой named pale things. Two words that started in different places and were slowly pulled apart into neighbouring territories of the spectrum.

The experiment

Winawer and colleagues, PNAS 2007: twenty blue chips spanning the range, shown three at a time; pick the two that match, at speed. Russian speakers were about 124 milliseconds faster when the odd chip sat across their голубой/синий boundary. English speakers showed no boundary effect at all. Make the Russians silently rehearse digit strings and the advantage disappears; load them with a spatial pattern instead and it survives. The categories live in the verbal channel.

That experiment is the reason these two words carry a whole literature on their backs. It is the cleanest demonstration in the colour domain of what linguistic relativity looks like when it is real: not different vision, but a habit of categorisation that runs so automatically it shows up in reaction times, and that can be switched off by keeping the inner voice busy. The authors' own summary is the best sentence in the field; the difference is not that English speakers cannot distinguish light from dark blue, but that Russian speakers cannot avoid distinguishing them.

It also comes with an afterlife that deserves to be told with it. In 2020, a team including Paramei herself ran Winawer-style discrimination tasks again and found no reaction-time advantage at the голубой/синий boundary; a speed advantage turned up instead at the hue boundary between голубой and green. The Russian blues, they concluded, are less well structured than previously thought. This does not undo the 2007 result, which stands as reported; it bounds it. The effect is real enough to publish and fragile enough to lose, which is the honest condition of most of this literature.

Russian is not alone in splitting blue, and the parallels sharpen the point. Greek opposes γαλάζιο, the light sky blue, to μπλε, the dark; a 2009 brain-potential study reported that Greek speakers' early visual responses registered the distinction even when colour was irrelevant to the task, though that finding too has since been challenged. Italian may go one better than Russian: in Florence, blu, azzurro and celeste all behave like basic terms in recent testing, three blues deep, a stratification the researchers trace to the city's medieval dyeing trade and Renaissance pigments. Turkish sits just short of the line; lacivert, the dark navy blue, is salient and frequent, but most speakers will tell you it is a kind of mavi, which by the standard criteria keeps it a shade rather than a term. The blue region of colour space, it turns out, is where languages most often decide to draw one line more.

Why blue, and why Russia? Paramei's answer is cultural accretion rather than optics: the sky-and-firmament blue of Orthodox icon painting, the dress blues of the imperial period, the paler word gathering associations of air and light while синий kept the sea and the night. None of that is a mechanism you could put in a laboratory, which is partly her point. A colour lexicon is a historical object. The perceptual machinery underneath is common property; where a language sets its fences on that common ground is a matter of record, trade and habit, and Russian happens to have fenced blue twice.

Which returns this note to the essay it serves. If you want the Sapir-Whorf question at its most concrete, it is here, in two ordinary Russian adjectives: a real, measured, milliseconds-sized effect of vocabulary on performance; a dependence on the verbal channel that shows the effect is habit, not hardware; and a failed replication sitting beside the famous result, keeping it honest. Language did not give Russians different eyes. It gave them a distinction they cannot put down.
