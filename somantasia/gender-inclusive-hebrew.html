Degendering Hebrew

In Hebrew you cannot say "you are reading" without announcing the gender of the person you are talking to. Nouns are gendered, adjectives agree, verbs agree in most of their forms, and the second person splits in two: atah to a man, at to a woman. There is no neutral. For a mixed room, or an unknown reader, the grammar's traditional answer is the masculine plural, which officially includes everyone and visibly names only the men. What happens when speakers of such a language decide that this default no longer describes the world they want to address? Hebrew has become one of the best live experiments there is.

The pressure arrived earliest through the law. Since 1988, Israeli employment law has required job advertisements to address both genders, which is why Israeli listings carry the slashed form דרוש/ה, "wanted", masculine and feminine fused at the suffix. From there the convention spread on its own: school notices addressing תלמידים.ות, students in both endings joined by a full stop; clinic forms, union circulars, university tenders. The punctuation is doing grammatical work, holding two inflections in one word. The Academy of the Hebrew Language, the state's arbiter of usage, disapproves; its guidance holds that the masculine form is unmarked, serving "not only for males", that periods and slashes inside words fall outside the rules of Hebrew writing, and that a writer wanting inclusion should write both full forms, students-masculine and students-feminine, the long way. The Academy has also had to deny, on its own website, a rumour that it had permitted feminine address for majority-female groups. It has ruled no such thing.

Whether the unmarked masculine is truly neutral is, usefully, an empirical question, and Hebrew has supplied real data. In a 2021 experiment published in npj Science of Learning, nearly a thousand Hebrew speakers sat a mathematics test with instructions randomly phrased in the masculine or the feminine. Women addressed in the feminine performed measurably better, and the gender gap in scores shrank by about a third. An earlier study had found that masculine-generic wording on questionnaires shifts how respondents describe themselves. Effects like these are the serious answer to the shrug that grammar is only grammar; address turns out to be a variable, and it can be moved.

Twelve new letters

In 2019 the designer Michal Shomer built the problem a typeface. Multi-gender Hebrew adds twelve letterforms that merge the masculine and feminine endings into single glyphs, so a word reads as both at once. It has appeared on school walls, wedding invitations, Knesset offices and vaccination centres, drawn tens of thousands of downloads, and been banned from religious state schools; adopted, attacked and rolled back, sometimes by the same institution. A font, carrying a constitutional argument.

The deeper reach for reform goes past merging the two genders toward adding a third. The Nonbinary Hebrew Project, built in 2018 by Lior Gross and Eyal Rivlin at the University of Colorado, proposes a full third paradigm on the vowel ending -eh: talmideh for a student, ateh for "you", running through verbs, adjectives and possessives on the pattern of the existing grammar, with a fused plural, talmidimot, that stitches the masculine and feminine plurals together. Its creators anchor the system in old ground: classical rabbinic law discusses people who fit neither category cleanly, the androgynos, the tumtum and others, categories concerned with bodies and legal obligation rather than identity, but proof at least that Jewish law has spent two millennia aware that not every person slots into two boxes. Reading those categories as ancient nonbinary gender is an interpretive choice, and a contested one; the textual fact that the categories exist is not.

The project's reception maps the territory neatly. In North American Jewish settings, camps, synagogues and classrooms, the system has found real use. In Israel it remains close to unknown; native speakers reaching for nonbinary language more often improvise with the tools already in the language, mixing masculine and feminine forms within a sentence, alternating by the day, or routing around gender with impersonal constructions, strategies documented among genderqueer Israelis well before any formal proposal. A youth movement coined its own suffix. Queer communities fused the plurals into chaverimot, friends of all endings, long before a university project systematised the idea. The Academy's senior researcher dismissed the third paradigm flatly: new words have rules, and this is not how Hebrew makes them. The reformers' answer, implicit in every slashed suffix on an Israeli bus shelter, is that usage has never waited for the Academy.

The liturgy travelled this road first. Marcia Falk's Book of Blessings, in 1996, rewrote Hebrew prayer from inside, composing new blessings that step away from the masculine sovereign address of the tradition; the arguments were vehement, and passages from it now sit in the prayer books of every major non-Orthodox movement. The American Reform siddur of 2007 dropped the masculine pronoun for God in English and set the four matriarchs beside the patriarchs in the standing prayer; some congregations call people to the Torah as mi-beit, "of the house of", where the traditional formula says son-of or daughter-of. Liturgical Hebrew turns out to be easier to reform than street Hebrew; it is rewritten deliberately, by committees, whereas the street changes by ten million small decisions.

The state pushed back in the summer of 2023, when the Civil Service Commissioner directed that official documents use masculine forms only, citing the Academy's position that the masculine serves as neutral. Three major universities publicly refused; an art academy answered by announcing a month of feminine-only forms in its publications. The episode is worth savouring as sociolinguistics: a government instructing its clerks that one gender is neutral, universities declining on the evidence that it is not, and a design school demonstrating, by inversion, exactly how neutral the default feels when it points the other way.

No one knows which of these solutions Hebrew will keep. Languages do absorb engineered change; modern Hebrew is itself the standing proof, a vernacular rebuilt from liturgical bone within living memory, which is partly why its speakers argue about deliberate reform with so little embarrassment. The gendered machinery runs deeper than vocabulary, into the agreement system itself, and the contenders are all still on the table: the long double forms the Academy prefers, the punctuation splices it rejects, a typeface that fuses the endings, a third paradigm waiting mostly abroad, and the oldest strategy of all, mixing the forms and letting the listener adjust. What the case already demonstrates is the thing this section keeps returning to: a grammar is not a neutral container. It is a record of who a language expected to be speaking, and to whom; and when the expectation changes, the record gets edited, one suffix at a time.
