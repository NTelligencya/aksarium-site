Where languages keep gender

English speakers who argue about inclusive language are arguing about a very small number of words. Four pronouns, a handful of possessives, some job titles that used to end in -ess. Almost nothing else in English carries gender at all: the verb does not change, the adjective does not change, the article does not change, the noun does not change. Whatever else is true of the English argument, it is being conducted over a thin seam in an otherwise gender-blind grammar. This is not the normal condition of human language, and it is worth understanding what the same argument looks like in languages where the seam runs through everything.

What follows is a tour. It starts with the hardest cases, the Semitic languages, where gender is not a feature of certain words but a property of nearly every clause; then it moves through languages that keep gender somewhere quite different, or nowhere at all. The point is not to rank grammars by how enlightened they are. It is to show that the question "how do we make this language more inclusive?" has a different shape in each place, because each language keeps its gender in a different room of the house.

Before the tour, one piece of vocabulary. English has two words where most languages have one. Sex refers to biological characteristics; gender to the social attributes associated with being male or female, learned through socialisation, which determine a person's position and value in a given context. That distinction is doing enormous work in English-language policy documents, and it does not survive translation intact. Arabic جِنْس jins covers both, which is why the sentence "sex and gender are different" comes back from Arabic as either "sex and sex are different" or "gender and gender are different", depending on which way the translator jumped. Thai เพศ phet covers sex, gender and sexuality together in ordinary usage. The United Nations dealt with its own version of the problem by commissioning a calque, النوع الاجتماعي al-naw' al-ijtima'i, "the social type", which now appears throughout Arabic policy translation and almost nowhere in Arabic conversation. A concept has to have somewhere to land.

Arabic, where the grammar will not let you stay quiet

Arabic assigns gender to nearly everything a speaker does. Nouns are masculine or feminine; adjectives agree; verbs agree in both the perfect and the imperfect, marking the gender of the subject and, in the second person, of the person being addressed. There is no neutral option, and no way to route around it, because the marking is not carried by a separate word you could decline to use. It is carried by the prefix and the suffix of the verb itself.

Start with the pronouns. Modern Standard Arabic has twelve, which already says something: English manages with seven, and Arabic needs twelve because it splits the second and third person by gender and adds a dual number for exactly two people.

The pronoun is the easy part, though, because Arabic mostly drops it. The gender is carried by the verb, and the verb cannot be dropped. Here is one ordinary verb, ذَهَبَ dhahaba, "to go", in its second and third persons.

"You went" is a different word depending on whether the person you are speaking to is a man, a woman, two people, a group of men, or a group of women. There is no sixth option. The dual row is the only place in the whole paradigm where a person can be addressed without the grammar committing to a gender, and it applies only if there happen to be exactly two of them.

The system is not tidy, either. تَذْهَبُ tadhhabu is simultaneously "you (one man) go" and "she goes"; تَذْهَبَانِ tadhhabani is simultaneously "you two go" and "those two women go". A grammar this insistent about gender still produces forms that collapse two people into one shape. Precision and obsession are not the same thing.

The part of Arabic that stops a room, though, is the relative pronoun. English has "who", "which" and "that", and none of them cares who you are. Arabic requires the relative pronoun to agree with its antecedent in gender, in number, and in the dual, in case as well.

This is the table that does the work in a room of English speakers. Most will accept that a language genders its nouns; they have met French. What they have usually not considered is that a language might require the word who to declare the sex of the person it refers to, so that "the student who arrived" and "the student who arrived" are two different sentences.

Arabic does hold neutral relativisers, which is worth knowing before anyone concludes the language is uniquely hostile. مَنْ man, "who", used of people, and مَا ma, "what", used of things, are invariable for gender and number. Classical grammars also list الأُلَى al-ula as a common-gender plural relative; it is archaic, largely confined to poetry, and almost nobody uses it, but it exists. The reform question in Arabic is less "why is there no neutral form" than "why did the neutral forms lose".

What speakers actually do

The workaround most often proposed is the dual, هما and أنتما, on the grounds that these two pronouns do not mark gender. Queer Arabic-speaking communities have promoted it, and it has two problems. The first is practical: Modern Standard Arabic keeps the dual, but most spoken dialects have discarded it everywhere except on nouns, so the form sounds either very formal or simply wrong. The second is conceptual, and the sharpest statement of it comes from inside Arabic scholarship rather than outside. Writing in the Beirut journal Kohl in 2022, Rola Alaedine points out that the dual denotes exactly two people, so using it of a non-binary individual describes one person as two. Escaping a binary by way of a duality is not obviously an improvement.

Alaedine works through the other options and rejects each: the masculine plural reinforces the default it is meant to soften; pairing هو/هي through a paragraph produces text nobody can read; transliterated English neopronouns invite exactly the accusation that the whole project is a Western import. Her conclusion is that new forms have to arrive through use rather than through committee, which is both correct and unsatisfying, and is roughly where the matter sits.

Two things are happening anyway, without anyone designing them. In urban Tunisian Arabic the historically feminine second-person singular إنتِي inti is used to address men and women alike, with the gender distinction in the verb lost along with it; the same generalisation is attested in northern Moroccan varieties. It is a long-standing dialectal development rather than an act of political reorganisation, whatever it later gets recruited for, and it does demonstrate that the second-person gender split is not load-bearing. Separately, most sedentary dialects have quietly dropped the distinct feminine plural in verbs and pronouns, which is a real reduction in gender-marking arrived at the usual way: by generalising the masculine.

The vocabulary problem, which is worse than the grammar

Alongside the grammar sits a lexical problem that no amount of paradigm design will fix. The standard reference term for transgender is مُتَحَوِّل جِنْسِيًّا mutahawwil jinsiyyan, built on a root meaning to change, turn or convert. Fed back through a machine translator, the bare participle returns some instructive English.

Community usage has largely moved to عابِر and عابِرة 'abir, 'abira, "crossing", "passing", and at least one Arabic reference now describes the older term as a slur.

The same pattern runs through the older vocabulary. شاذ shadh, "deviant" or "anomalous", was the standard media word for gay until activists in Lebanon spent years replacing it with مثلي and مثلية mithli, mithliyya, "same", which is now standard in much Arabic media. The community itself is مجتمع الميم mujtama' al-meem, the meem community, named for the Arabic letter M that begins each of the component words. And searching a major bilingual dictionary for cisgender still returns no exact match in Arabic to English. Some concepts have not yet been given a place to land, and the work of giving them one is being done in Cairo, Beirut and Tunis rather than anywhere else.

Georgetown, March 2021

A first-year Arabic student at Georgetown published a piece in the student paper arguing that the university should open a conversation about gender-inclusive Arabic in its language courses. He did not propose new pronouns; he asked for a discussion. The comment thread, still visible, ran to roughly forty replies and was overwhelmingly hostile, and the charge was consistent: cultural imperialism, a white saviour complex, an American demanding that a language he had studied for six months rearrange itself for his comfort. "Leave Semitic languages alone", wrote one commenter identifying as a Hebrew speaker; "it's for local speakers to deal with." The episode is worth keeping in view for the rest of this tour, because it is the objection most likely to be raised and the one the record handles least kindly. The Arabic gender wiki is Egyptian. The Arabic gender dictionary is Lebanese. The sharpest published critique of the dual-as-neutral proposal was written by an Arabic-speaking scholar in a Beirut journal. And as the Chinese section below shows, the first recorded feminist objection to a gendered pronoun was published in Shanghai in 1929.

Hebrew, the same machinery and a different politics

Hebrew works like Arabic and has been through far more institutional argument, which makes it the best-documented live experiment in the group. You cannot say "you are reading" in Hebrew without announcing the gender of the person you are talking to: אתה atah to a man, את at to a woman. Nouns are gendered, adjectives agree, verbs agree in most of their forms. For a mixed room, or an unknown reader, the traditional answer is the masculine plural, which officially includes everyone and visibly names only the men.

The pressure arrived earliest through the law. Since 1988, Israeli employment law has required job advertisements to address both genders, which is why Israeli listings carry the slashed form דרוש/ה, "wanted", masculine and feminine fused at the suffix. From there the convention spread on its own: school notices addressing תלמידים.ות, students in both endings joined by a full stop; clinic forms, union circulars, university tenders. The punctuation is doing grammatical work, holding two inflections in one word. The Academy of the Hebrew Language, the state's arbiter of usage, disapproves; its guidance holds that the masculine form is unmarked, serving "not only for males", that periods and slashes inside words fall outside the rules of Hebrew writing, and that a writer wanting inclusion should write both full forms the long way.

Whether the unmarked masculine is truly neutral is, usefully, an empirical question, and Hebrew has supplied real data. In a 2021 experiment published in npj Science of Learning, nearly a thousand Hebrew speakers sat a mathematics test with instructions randomly phrased in the masculine or the feminine. Women addressed in the feminine performed measurably better, and the gender gap in scores shrank by about a third. An earlier study had found that masculine-generic wording on questionnaires shifts how respondents describe themselves. Effects like these are the serious answer to the shrug that grammar is only grammar; address turns out to be a variable, and it can be moved.

The deeper reach for reform goes past merging the two genders toward adding a third. The Nonbinary Hebrew Project, built in 2018 by Lior Gross and Eyal Rivlin at the University of Colorado, proposes a full third paradigm on the vowel ending -eh, running through pronouns, nouns, verbs, adjectives and possessives on the pattern of the existing grammar.

The plural is a splice: -im, the masculine plural ending, welded to -ot, the feminine, giving -imot. The same splice was arrived at independently by an American Jewish summer camp, which coined חניכול chanichol for a camper of unspecified gender, and by Israeli queer communities, who had been saying חברימות chaverimot, friends of all endings, well before any university systematised the idea.

The project's creators anchor the system in old ground: classical rabbinic law discusses people who fit neither category cleanly, the androgynos, the tumtum and others, categories concerned with bodies and legal obligation rather than identity, but proof at least that Jewish law has spent two millennia aware that not every person slots into two boxes. Reading those categories as ancient non-binary gender is an interpretive choice, and a contested one; the textual fact that the categories exist is not.

The reception maps the territory neatly. In North American Jewish settings, camps, synagogues and classrooms, the system has found real use. In Israel it remains close to unknown; native speakers reaching for non-binary language more often improvise with the tools already in the language, mixing masculine and feminine forms within a sentence, alternating by the day, or routing around gender with impersonal constructions, strategies documented among genderqueer Israelis well before any formal proposal. The Academy's senior researcher dismissed the third paradigm flatly: new words have rules, and this is not how Hebrew makes them.

Hebrew has also produced the one intervention nobody else has tried. In 2019 the designer Michal Shomer built the problem a typeface. Multi-gender Hebrew adds twelve letterforms that merge the masculine and feminine endings into single glyphs, so a word reads as both at once. It has appeared on school walls, wedding invitations, Knesset offices and vaccination centres, drawn tens of thousands of downloads, and been banned from religious state schools; adopted, attacked and rolled back, sometimes by the same institution. A font, carrying a constitutional argument.

The state pushed back in the summer of 2023, when the Civil Service Commissioner directed that official documents use masculine forms only, citing the Academy's position that the masculine serves as neutral. Three major universities publicly refused; an art academy answered by announcing a month of feminine-only forms in its publications. The episode is worth savouring as sociolinguistics: a government instructing its clerks that one gender is neutral, universities declining on the evidence that it is not, and a design school demonstrating, by inversion, exactly how neutral the default feels when it points the other way.

Thai, where you gender yourself

Now the tour turns, because Thai puts gender somewhere Arabic and Hebrew do not: on the speaker. Thai first-person pronouns are chosen according to the sex, age and social position of the person talking. The consequence is structural, and rather elegant. A Thai speaker declares their own gender constantly and cannot easily be misgendered by anyone else, because the second and third persons carry almost no gender at all.

Sources disagree about กู: it is unmarked for gender in the standard pronoun tables, but at least one teaching source describes it as male-skewed in practice. Note the shape of the problem. The neutral options all exist and are all marked for something else, usually informality or ceremony, so a Thai speaker who wants to be polite and ungendered at the same time has nowhere obvious to stand.

The third person, meanwhile, is genuinely neutral. เขา khao is the ordinary word for both "he" and "she". Written Thai sometimes distinguishes เธอ thoe as "she", but that is largely a convention of translated fiction and song lyrics rather than of speech; in conversation เธอ is a second-person pronoun, and an unusual one, because it is semi-feminine in a way that genders the pair rather than either party. It is used when the speaker or the listener, or both, are female.

Where Thai does gender relentlessly is at the end of the sentence. Polite Thai attaches a final particle, and the particle depends on the sex of the speaker: ครับ khrap from a man, ค่ะ kha from a woman, shifting to คะ with a high tone in questions and requests. These attach to almost every polite utterance. A Thai speaker therefore announces their gender several times per conversation, not through pronouns at all but through the tail of each sentence, and there is no polite register that omits them.

Because phet covers all three domains at once, Thai treats these as nouns naming categories rather than as modifiers stacked on a gendered head; where English says "gay man", Thai names a category outright. Anthropologists working on this, Dredge Käng among them, argue that phet categories operate multidimensionally, referencing anatomy, presentation, desire and moral standing simultaneously, and are therefore not equivalent to Western sexuality terms even where the dictionary offers a one-to-one gloss. Whether that constitutes a genuinely different folk model of gender, or an artefact of translation, is contested in the field, which is itself the useful lesson.

One coda. Thailand's Marriage Equality Act took effect in January 2025, and more than 1,750 same-sex couples married on the first day. A gender recognition bill was rejected by the House of Representatives in February 2024 and, on the sources available to me, has not passed since. So Thailand has same-sex marriage and no legal gender change, which sharpens the linguistic point rather than softening it: the polite particles keep declaring a gender the state will not let you amend.

Mandarin, where gender exists only on the page

Mandarin is the mirror image of Arabic. It has no grammatical gender at all: no agreement on nouns, adjectives or verbs, and no inflection for agreement to sit in. The entire question reduces to a small closed set of written characters, every one of which is pronounced identically.

Every one of these is said tā, first tone. The distinction does not exist in speech. Spoken Mandarin cannot misgender in the third person because it cannot gender in the third person; written Mandarin must choose, on every single occurrence.

The feminine character has a history that repays attention, because it is the same argument conducted a century early. Before about 1918, 他 covered everybody. The proposal to split it came from the poet and linguist Liu Bannong, announced publicly by Zhou Zuoren in the journal New Youth in August 1918, and it was contested at once. The rival candidate was 伊 yi, the ordinary third-person pronoun of Shanghainese, backed for a time by Zhou Zuoren and Qian Xuantong; a critic writing as Hanbing published a piece in 1920 titled "This is Liu Bannong's Fault"; Liu answered with an essay defending the character; and by the 1930s 她 had won, helped along by a popular song for which he had written the lyrics.

The objection that matters came later, and from the direction least expected. The magazine Funü Gongming, "Women's Resonance", founded in 1929, refused on principle to print 她. Its argument was structural: 他 is built on the person radical 亻, and 她 replaces it with the woman radical 女. Men got to be people; women got to be women. An article of the same year argued that women's liberation should be working to eliminate gender distinctions in language rather than to invent new ones, so that both sexes could simply be persons. A character created to make women visible in writing was rejected by a feminist magazine precisely because it made them visible, and visible as not-quite-human. That is the whole modern argument, in Shanghai, ninety-seven years ago.

The woman radical carries a broader complaint. It appears in a conspicuous number of characters with unpleasant meanings: 奸 adultery and treachery, 嫉 and 妒 jealousy and envy, 妖 monster or witch, 妓 and 娼 prostitute. Quantitative claims about this circulate and should be treated with suspicion, since nobody has defined what counts as derogatory; the pattern is real, the percentages are invented. An exhibition built around the character 姦, three women stacked, meaning wickedness, was banned by the authorities in November 2015. There is domestic precedent for the reform being asked for: in the early years of the People's Republic the government altered characters used for the names of minority ethnic groups so they no longer carried "insect" or "dog" radicals. What there is not, so far as I could establish, is an organised campaign to do the same for 女.

The modern proposals are correspondingly narrow, because Mandarin's problem is narrow. Writing "TA" in Latin letters became the mainland default for neutral reference from around 2013; it works, and it makes some speakers uncomfortable for the obvious reason that it embeds an alphabet in the middle of a Chinese sentence. The alternative is a new character, X也, created in 2015 by a Chinese intersex information platform: an X-shaped element over 也, the phonetic component shared by all the existing forms. The X carries no sound. It cannot; the character is pronounced tā like every other third-person pronoun, so it exists only in writing, which is exactly where the problem is. A Unicode application submitted in late 2020 was approved in September 2025, and implementation across fonts and input methods is still working through. There is also a quieter option, which is to revive 伊, the character that lost in 1920, as a neutral form precisely because it never won a gender.

Japanese, choosing a self

Japanese resembles Thai more than anything else in this tour. It has no grammatical gender and no agreement, and it locates gender where Thai does: in the choice of first-person pronoun, which is a small act of self-presentation performed several times a day.

Non-binary Japanese speakers interviewed in 2022 described using watashi at work as a screen that gives nothing away, and jibun privately as a statement. The neutral options here are genuinely neutral, and there are three of them, which is more than Thai polite speech offers.

Japanese also sidesteps the third-person problem structurally rather than by reform. 彼 kare and 彼女 kanojo exist, but they are recent and were built to order. In pre-modern Japanese kare was a distal demonstrative used of men, women and things alike; contact with European languages created a demand for a masculine and feminine third-person split, at which point kare narrowed to males and kanojo was assembled from kano onna, "that woman", crystallising into a single word only from the Taishō period. Both now also mean "boyfriend" and "girlfriend", which is one reason speakers avoid them. In ordinary conversation Japanese uses names with suffixes, kinship terms, job titles, or simply drops the argument, which the grammar permits freely. It is worth noticing that a language can retire its gendered pronouns by not using them, and that no committee is required.

Japan's own non-binary vocabulary developed locally and early. Xジェンダー x-jendā, X-gender, emerged in the Kansai region in the late 1990s through publications and independent documentaries made by transgender people, and was first formally defined in a magazine glossary in 2000. It comes with its own subdivisions: 両性 ryōsei, both; 中性 chūsei, neutral; 無性 musei, none; 不定性 futeisei, fluid. Scholars of the term stress that although it is often glossed as "genderqueer", it is a specifically Japanese construct shaped by Japanese medical and activist history rather than an import.

The Romance repair jobs

Spanish and French are where the argument has become properly institutional, complete with academies, circulars, court rulings and a bill before the Senate. Both are gendered throughout, both take the masculine plural as the default for mixed groups, and both have produced a set of typographic devices for holding two forms in one word.

The Spanish academies have said no, repeatedly and in writing. The Real Academia Española's Libro de estilo, published in November 2018, explicitly rejects @, x and e as devices for referring to both sexes, and generally rejects doubling as well. Its Informe sobre el lenguaje inclusivo, requested by the Spanish government in 2018 and approved in January 2020, concluded that the 1978 Constitution needed no revision and defended the masculine as the unmarked term. The one moment of apparent movement was a misunderstanding: in October 2020 the RAE added elle to its Observatorio de palabras, a watch-list explicitly carrying no normative endorsement, and pulled the entry about a week later, stating that the confusion generated by its presence made removal preferable.

The pushback has since become governmental. Buenos Aires city banned the e, x and @ forms in teaching materials and school communications in June 2022. In February 2024 the Argentine Ministry of Defence prohibited inclusive language across the ministry and the armed forces by formal resolution, on the stated ground that non-standard variation risks misinterpretation of orders, and the government announced days later that the prohibition would be extended across the national public administration.

The usage data is instructive, and it does not flatter the American coinage. Pew Research Center surveys of US Hispanic adults found awareness of Latinx rising from 23 per cent in 2019 to 47 per cent by late 2023, while actual use went from 3 per cent to 4 per cent, a change Pew describes as statistically unchanged. Only 18 per cent had heard of Latine, the alternative that actually fits Spanish morphology, since -e is already a neutral nominal ending in words like estudiante. Eighty-one per cent prefer Hispanic or Latino. Awareness clusters among the US-born, English-dominant and college-educated, which is the demographic signature the critics predicted. Worth setting against that: the Modern Language Association's guidance cautions that "few people use it" is a weak argument, because it implies everyone must accept the categories of the majority.

France has produced the strangest position of the group, which is to ban the typography while mandating the feminisation. The Académie française declared unanimously in October 2017 that faced with this "inclusive aberration" the French language now stood en péril mortel. The following month Prime Minister Édouard Philippe circulated an instruction not to use inclusive writing in texts destined for official publication, and in the same document required systematic feminisation of job titles, ranks and functions held by women. The education ministry repeated the split in May 2021: no point médian in schools, and teachers required to feminise job titles and use gender-neutral formulations. The Conseil d'État upheld that circular in December 2024. In February 2019 the Académie itself accepted, by a large majority, that there is no obstacle in principle to feminising the names of trades and functions, a marked softening of its earlier hostility.

The Senate voted in October 2023, by 221 to 82, for a bill banning inclusive writing in education and in public bodies, with legal acts containing it rendered void, and extended the ban to neologisms including iel. It has never been voted on by the Assemblée nationale and remains at committee stage. So it is not correct to say that inclusive writing is banned by law in France; it is banned by administrative circular, upheld by the courts, in state publications and in schools.

One historical claim in this argument deserves care, because it is repeated everywhere. It is true that agreement with the nearest conjunct, the règle de proximité, was normal in Latin, Old French and Renaissance French, and it is true that named grammarians justified the change in frankly sexist terms: Dupleix in 1651 wrote that the masculine gender is the more noble and prevails alone against two or more feminines; Bouhours in 1675 that when the two genders meet, the nobler must win; Beauzée in 1767 grounded it in the superiority of the male over the female. Those quotations are real. The stronger claim built on them, that a coordinated seventeenth-century masculinist project abolished a rule which had been the rule, is contested in the scholarship, with a peer-reviewed rebuttal from Sophie Piron in 2022 and corpus work showing proximity agreement is still common in French today. The grammarians' reasoning is quotable; the conspiracy is not established.

Swedish, the one that worked

Only one engineered pronoun in this tour has travelled from proposal to dictionary to ordinary use within living memory, and it repays study because the timeline undercuts the tidy story in both directions.

The attitude data is the useful part. Researchers tracked Swedish opinion at six points between 2012 and 2015 and found it inverted: in 2012, 56 per cent were very negative and 17 per cent very positive; by 2015, 69 per cent were very positive and 10 per cent very negative. Time remained one of the strongest predictors even after controlling for sexism, political orientation, age and gender. Behaviour lagged: those who never or almost never used the word fell from half the sample to a quarter, while consistent users stayed at around one in eight. A follow-up on representative samples found roughly half the Swedish population reporting some use by 2018, up fourteen points on 2015, with the largest positive shift among the young.

A separate eye-tracking study addressed the commonest objection directly, that new pronouns are hard to read. The measured cost was 43 milliseconds, in the region after the pronoun rather than on it, with comprehension unaffected at 93 per cent accuracy. Whatever the case against engineered pronouns is, it is not a processing cost.

Sweden is also where the argument for linguistic reform as a lever on social attitudes was made most strongly, in a 2019 study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences: speakers prompted to use hen were less likely to bring men to mind, more likely to name a woman as a hypothetical political candidate, and expressed more favourable views of women and of LGBT people in public life. It is the study the Georgetown student cited, and it is worth reading alongside its best objection, made by Dennis Baron a few weeks later: Sweden acquired its reputation for gender parity during roughly the fifty years when hen lay dormant. The social change preceded the linguistic one by half a century.

The languages that never had any of this

Something like half the world's languages have no grammatical gender at all. In the standard typological survey, 145 of 256 sampled languages have no gender system, using the test that matters, which is whether gender shows up as agreement on other words rather than merely as separate items of vocabulary. Gender-marking is the minority pattern globally, not the default.

English does not belong in this table. In the typology used by the quantitative literature it is a "natural gender" language: gendered third-person pronouns, no gender agreement anywhere else. Basque nearly belongs but not quite, because the allocutive verb forms in some dialects mark the sex of the person being addressed.

This set is where the argument about whether grammar matters has to be settled, and the honest answer is that the evidence does not support the causal story in either direction. The paper usually cited to prove that genderless languages produce better outcomes does not say that. Prewitt-Freilino, Caswell and Laakso compared 111 countries in 2012 and found that gendered-language countries showed less gender equality than others, but that the highest scores belonged to natural gender countries, with genderless-language countries in the middle. Their explanation is worth sitting with: where a language marks gender visibly but lightly, sexist usage is easy to see and easy to repair, whereas in a genderless language a male default can travel unchallenged under cover of apparently neutral terms. That is not merely speculative. A 2023 study tested exactly this and reported a male bias in the supposedly neutral pronouns of Turkish and Finnish.

The economic literature points the other way and is careful about it. Jakiela and Ozier coded 4,336 languages and found grammatical gender associated with an 11.9 percentage-point decline in female labour force participation across 178 countries, with individual-level effects of similar magnitude in four African countries. Their own caveat is the best statement of the problem: these are correlations; modern gender attitudes cannot have caused ancient grammar, but culture could plausibly have shaped both, and no experiment is possible at this level. The general methodological warning is the 2013 PLOS ONE paper in which two researchers deliberately constructed a statistically significant chain linking linguistic diversity to traffic fatalities, to demonstrate that large cross-cultural databases will yield rigorous-looking correlations for anything at all, because languages are not independent observations.

The simplest check is to look at where the genderless-language countries actually sit. On the World Economic Forum's 2025 index, Finland is second and Iran is 145th; Japan is 118th, Georgia 63rd, Estonia 11th. Persian, Turkish, Georgian, Finnish, Estonian, Japanese and Korean are all genderless, and their countries occupy essentially the entire range. Grammatical gender does not predict outcomes within the genderless set at all. Meanwhile Iceland, which has a full three-gender system, ranks first. The variance is dominated by law, economy, religion and politics; the grammar is somewhere far down the list. Whoever in the Georgetown comment thread suggested the student simply switch to Farsi had a point about the grammar and no point at all about the society.

What the tour shows

Three things, none of them visible from inside English.

The first is that these languages are not solving the same problem. Arabic and Hebrew put gender on the person being addressed and then run it through the whole grammar, which is why their reform proposals are enormous, a third paradigm or a new alphabet, and why the proposals mostly fail. Thai and Japanese put it on the person speaking, which makes misgendering by others structurally difficult and self-description unavoidable. Mandarin puts it in the orthography alone, which is why one new character and a Unicode application very nearly finish the job. Asking "is this language inclusive?" without asking "where does it keep gender?" produces answers that are not about anything.

The second is that every language here already contains the neutral form it is said to lack, and has marginalised it rather than never having had it. Arabic has مَنْ and the archaic الأُلَى, and a dual the spoken dialects discarded. Mandarin has spoken tā, which is perfectly neutral, and 伊, which lost a contest in 1920. Thai has เขา and three neutral ways of saying "I", all marked for something else. Japanese has three, and uses them. The reform question is more often "why did the neutral form lose" than "how do we build one".

The third is the one the Georgetown thread got wrong. The charge that gender-inclusive language is an Anglophone export does not survive contact with the record. The Arabic gender wiki was founded in Cairo; the Arabic gender dictionary in Beirut; the neutral Chinese character by a Chinese intersex platform; X-gender in Kansai in the 1990s; the fused Hebrew plural by Israeli queer communities before any American systematised it. And the earliest recorded feminist objection to a gendered pronoun anywhere in this tour was published in a Shanghai women's magazine in 1929, arguing that liberation meant eliminating the distinction rather than elaborating it, half a century before the English-speaking world arrived at the same thought. The argument travels, certainly. It does not have a single point of origin, and it never has.

What none of this settles is whether any of it changes anything, and the evidence there is genuinely mixed rather than politely balanced. Hebrew gives the strongest positive result: address women in the feminine and their mathematics scores rise measurably. Swedish gives the strongest negative one: the country built its reputation for equality during the fifty years its famous pronoun sat unused. Both are true. A grammar is not a neutral container; it is a record of who a language expected to be speaking, and to whom. Editing the record is worth doing. It is not the same thing as changing what was recorded.

On the person addressed. Arabic, Hebrew. The second person splits and the verb agrees, so reform means rebuilding the agreement system. On the person speaking. Thai, Japanese. First-person pronouns, and in Thai the sentence-final particles. Misgendering by others is structurally difficult. On the noun and everything touching it. Spanish, French.
