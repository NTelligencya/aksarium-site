The IPA

Every alphabet on this site lies about sound, each in its own way. The International Phonetic Alphabet is the repair: one symbol per sound, for every language a human mouth has produced. Six short modules; by the end you can read the transcriptions in any dictionary on your shelf.

Module 01

One sound, one symbol

Myth we'll unpick: "Letters tell you how words sound"

Ordinary spelling records history, politics and habit; only occasionally sound. The IPA was invented to write down what the mouth actually does.

The alphabet is an unreliable witness

Take four English letters: ough. Now listen to them in though, through, tough, cough, thought and bough: six words, six different sounds, one spelling. Run it the other way and it is no better; the sound ee arrives dressed as e, ee, ea, ie, ei, ey and y.

English is an extreme case, but no ordinary orthography escapes. Spelling is frozen history; pronunciation kept moving after the ice set.

So the phoneticians built their own

In Paris in 1886, a circle of language teachers around Paul Passy founded what became the International Phonetic Association, and set about building an alphabet where the deal is absolute: one symbol, one sound, always. No silent letters, no context rules, no exceptions. The chart has been revised ever since; slowly, by vote, like the treaty it is.

The association and the alphabet share the abbreviation IPA, which is occasionally confusing even to phoneticians. The alphabet's core is roughly a hundred letters, extended by diacritics; most single languages use only two or three dozen of them.

Module 02

Where sounds are made

Myth we'll unpick: "A consonant is just a noise"

Every consonant is an address with three lines: where the air is obstructed, how it is obstructed, and whether the voice is switched on. The IPA chart is simply the street map.

Three questions, any consonant

Put your fingers on your throat and hold a long sss, then a long zzz. Same tongue, same teeth, same air; the only difference is the buzz under your fingers. That buzz is voicing, the third line of the address. The first two are place (lips, teeth, ridge, soft palate, throat) and manner (full stop, hiss, hum through the nose).

s and z are a minimal pair of the machine itself: identical place, identical manner, voicing flipped. The chart you are about to meet arranges every consonant by exactly these three lines.

A working slice of the chart

Place runs left to right, front of the mouth to the throat · click a cell

Where a cell holds two symbols, the left is voiceless and the right voiced; the IPA's own convention. This is a teaching slice, not the full chart; the real one adds retroflex and palatal columns, clicks, implosives and more.

Addresses English never visits

ʕ
Arabic ع · ʿayn

A voiced pharyngeal fricative: the throat itself narrows. The sound learners of Arabic circle for months; on the chart it is just one column further back than anything English uses.

q
Arabic ق · qāf

A uvular stop: like k, but struck against the very back of the soft palate. The difference between k and q is pure place; manner and voicing are identical.

x
Russian х · Arabic خ

A voiceless velar fricative: a k relaxed into a hiss. Russian хлеб (bread), Arabic خ, German Bach, Scottish loch; common everywhere, absent from standard Australian English.

None of these is exotic to the chart; each is one step along a row you already know. That is the point of the map.

Module 03

The vowel space

Myth we'll unpick: "There are five vowels"

Five is the number of letters. Australian English alone has around twenty vowel sounds; the IPA maps them not as a list but as positions in the open space of the mouth.

A vowel is a position, not a letter

Say ee and hold it, then slide slowly to ah. Nothing stops the air; all that changes is where your tongue sits: high and forward, then low and back. Vowels differ along three sliders: tongue height, tongue backness, and whether the lips are rounded. Say ee again and round your lips as if for oo, changing nothing else: that is the French u, the German ü.

Because the sliders are continuous, the IPA pins reference points into the space; a trapezium of agreed positions, drawn to match the mouth's actual room to move: more space at the top than at the bottom.

Three vowels worth the detour

ə
schwa · the resting vowel

Dead centre of the map: the tongue at rest. The most common vowel in English; the a of "about", both vowels of "kangaroo" that carry no stress. Unstressed English collapses towards it.

y
French tu · German über

The ee-position with rounded lips; a front rounded vowel. English never rounds its front vowels, which is exactly why this one feels impossible for the first week of French.

ɨ
Russian ы

A close central vowel: ee pulled back to the middle of the mouth. The famous hurdle of Russian мы (we) and ты (you); on the map it is simply the top edge, halfway along.

Letters run out; positions do not. Now for everything the segments cannot carry.

Module 04

Stress, length and tone

Myth we'll unpick: "Get the consonants and vowels right and you're done"

Some of what a word means never touches a consonant or a vowel. It rides on top: which syllable is hit, how long a sound is held, where the pitch goes. The IPA writes all of it.

Stress: the hit that changes the word

English record is two words wearing one spelling: ˈrekɔːd the noun, rəˈkɔːd the verb; only the stress mark moves. Russian goes further: за́мок is a castle and замо́к is a lock, identical on the page in normal print. The IPA's ˈ sits before the stressed syllable, like a small upbeat.

Russian dictionaries mark stress with an acute accent precisely because the ordinary orthography refuses to; every Russian learner's misread of this pair is a stress error, not a spelling error. A lowered mark ˌ notes secondary stress.

Length: the colon that isn't

كَتَبَ
kataba · ˈkataba

He wrote. Three short a-vowels; the plain past verb from the root walked through in the Root and pattern deck.

كَاتَبَ
kātaba · ˈkaːtaba

He corresponded with someone. One held vowel, one changed meaning; in the IPA the whole difference is the length mark ː.

In Arabic, vowel length is load-bearing grammar. The mark that carries it, ː, is two triangles, not a colon; the fine print is next.

Module 05

The fine print

Myth we'll unpick: "The small marks are decoration"

Diacritics are where the IPA stops sketching and starts engraving. Some of them mark differences your ear has been trained since childhood to ignore.

The puff you never noticed

Hold your palm in front of your mouth and say pin, then spin. The p of pin lands with a small puff of air; the p of spin does not. English speakers cannot hear the difference because English never uses it to distinguish words; the IPA writes it anyway: pʰɪn against spɪn.

In Hindi and Urdu that same puff is vocabulary: aspirated and unaspirated consonants are different letters and different words. What one language treats as noise, another treats as meaning; the notation has to carry both.

Korean splits the atom three ways

달
tal · tal

Moon. The plain consonant: no puff, no tension, just the stop.

탈
tʰal · tʰal

Mask. The aspirated one; the pin-puff, promoted from noise to meaning.

딸
ttal · t͈al

Daughter. The tense one, made with a stiffened vocal tract; the IPA marks it with a small double line below.

Moon, mask, daughter: one consonant slot, three words. English ears hear one sound; Korean ears, and the IPA, hear three.

Six marks that do most of the work

ˈprimary stressBefore the stressed syllable: ˈrekɔːd. The single most useful mark in an English dictionary.
ːlengthThe sound before it is held: Arabic kaːtaba. Phonemic in Arabic, Japanese and Finnish, among many others.
ʰaspirationThe puff: pʰɪn. Meaningful in Hindi, Urdu, Korean, Thai and Mandarin; automatic in English.
ʲpalatalisationA y-flavour on the consonant. The soft consonants of Russian: брат brat is brother, брать bratʲ is to take; the soft sign made audible.
̃nasalisationAir through the nose as well: French bon bɔ̃. The reason French vowels sound French.
̥voicelessnessA ring beneath: the voice switched off where you would expect it on. How phoneticians write whispered or devoiced sounds.

Slashes and brackets

/ Slashes are broad: only the differences that change words in that language. English /pɪn/ ignores the puff, because English does. This is what dictionaries print.
[ Brackets are narrow: what the mouth actually did, puff and all: [pʰɪn]. This is what phoneticians write in the field, before they know which details matter.

Same word, two depths of focus. You now have the whole toolkit; time to read.

Module 06

Reading in the wild

The IPA is not a museum piece; it sits between slashes in every serious dictionary, on every Wikipedia pronunciation line, in every field linguist's notebook. Time to read it cold.

What a dictionary hands you

After the headword, between slashes, a good dictionary gives the broad transcription: the sounds that matter, the stress mark, the length marks, nothing else. Two habits unlock it: find the ˈ first and let it set the rhythm, then trust the symbols over the spelling; j is the y of yes, never the j of jam, and r-less endings are not missing anything.

Dictionaries of Australian English transcribe the non-rhotic vowels directly: no r after a vowel, its work done by length and quality instead. The transcriptions in the next beat are Australian.

Now you read

Australian English squashes its unstressed vowels to ə and drops r after vowels. This city of five letters' worth of sound:

/ˈmɛlbən/
Melbourne.Two syllables, not three; the "-bourne" is just bən. The spelling promises far more vowel than anyone delivers.

ŋ is the sing-sound and ˌ a lighter hit before the main ˈ. This animal:

/ˌkæŋɡəˈruː/
Kangaroo.From gangurru in Guugu Yimithirr, recorded at Endeavour River in 1770; note the unstressed middle syllable resting on schwa.

The length mark is doing the work an r once did. This city:

/ˈdaːwɪn/
Darwin.Non-rhotic Australian English: the r survives only as vowel length, exactly what ː is for.

Why this matters

Every language on this site arrives wrapped in a script you may not read yet. The IPA is the skeleton key: it lets you hold the sound of an Arabic verb, a Russian stress pair or a Thai tone in writing before the script itself has yielded. Learn these hundred-odd symbols once and every script section on this site, and every dictionary you will ever open, is already half unlocked.
