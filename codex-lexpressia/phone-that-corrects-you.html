Codex Lexpressia · An interactive walkthrough

The phone that corrects you

Nearly every adult in North Korea now carries a smartphone. It is a Chinese handset running a modified Android, and it has been rebuilt so thoroughly that the interface itself is an argument: every label on it is a Northern word, chosen against a Southern one. Then, in the messaging app, the argument stops being passive. Type a Southern word and the phone rewrites it while you watch. About fifteen minutes, and every claim is sourced.

Why a phone is the right object

The two Korean standards have been drifting apart since 1945, and the usual way to teach that is a table of word pairs. The trouble with a table is that it presents the divergence as something that happened, quietly, over decades. It did not only happen. Since 2023 it has been manufactured, enforced and prosecuted, and the enforcement now runs inside consumer software. A phone shows you the finished state and the machinery in the same object.

So this deck teaches the linguistics through the device rather than alongside it. You will read an application drawer in Munhwaeo and work out the Southern equivalents yourself; you will meet the file-signature system that decides which media may open at all; and you will type into a message box that will not accept what you typed. The vocabulary you leave with is real vocabulary. The architecture around it is the reason that vocabulary looks the way it does.

What a drawer of words is evidence of

None of these are dialect survivals. They are the output of 말다듬기, the language-refinement campaign that ran from 1964 under Kim Il-sung's directives, which set out to replace difficult Sino-Korean and foreign words with native Korean ones. The South ran its own purification campaign at the same time and largely failed at it, because it was advisory and the market ignored it. The North's succeeded because it was not advisory. A phone built in 2024 is where you can see the difference: the South says 브라우저, and the North says 열람기, and both of those are the result of a decision taken sixty years ago.

The clearest measure of relative success is the mobile phone itself. The North's native coinage 손전화, "hand phone", displaced the loanword; the South still says 휴대폰 and 핸드폰. In the other direction, the North's 1980s coinage 얼음보숭이 for ice cream never displaced 아이스크림 in ordinary speech, so the campaign's own record is uneven and the deck should not overstate it.

Module 02

What the phone requires of you

Module 01 read the surface. Underneath it sits an architecture that security researchers have taken apart twice: once on a tablet in 2016, and again on handsets in 2022 and 2024. Three mechanisms do most of the work. Every file must prove where it came from; the phone photographs its own screen and shows you that it has; and the price of being connected is paid in foreign currency.

Read the mechanism

The phone screenshots itself, and tells you so. Researchers who examined North Korean devices found a standard feature that captures the screen during use and records which application was open at the time. The user can see the timestamp and the application name for every capture. The user cannot view the images, and cannot delete them. They can be exported for an official to inspect.

The design intent is worth naming precisely, because "spyware" is the wrong word. Covert collection hides itself. This is deterrence by visibility: the log is shown to you on purpose, so that you moderate your own behaviour. Try the two buttons.

Note the app name itself: 열람리력, where Southern orthography would give 열람 이력. The initial sound rule is doing work in the name of the surveillance feature.

Three different phones, three different triggers

The capture behaviour is not one fact, and merging it into one would be a fabrication. On the Woolim tablet analysed in 2016, the capture fired when an application was opened. On the handsets examined for Project Reveal in 2022, the capture was described as random during use. On one Samtaesong handset smuggled out and examined in 2025, it ran on a fixed five-minute interval. Different devices, different software generations, five years apart.

This is a general lesson about open-source research on closed systems, and the same one Module 03 of the earlier deck makes about dated screenshots. Every finding is a photograph of one device at one moment. The honest sentence names the device and the year; the dishonest one says "North Korean phones do X".

Some unverified sources also report that the leaders' names are automatically emboldened wherever they appear in text. It is a widely repeated observation, and a plausible one given the same behaviour in Red Star OS, but it has not been confirmed in a published teardown, so it sits here as a claim rather than a finding.

Why this matters

A North Korean phone is not a cheap phone. Advertised handset prices in 2024 put two models at roughly US$395 and US$198, and every data plan carries a monthly charge payable in foreign-currency won, which ordinary wages are not paid in. The device is simultaneously a consumer object, a surveillance terminal and a mechanism for drawing hard currency out of households. All three at once, which is the part a single-sentence description always loses.

Module 03

The word the phone will not let you type

On 18 January 2023 North Korea passed the Pyongyang Cultural Language Protection Act: five chapters, sixty-five articles, criminalising South Korean vocabulary, grammar, writing style and intonation. Article 19 bans "puppet-style" address terms and requires 동지 or 동무 instead. Article 22 bans the rising phrase-final intonation. Two years later, a handset examined in Pyongyang carried the Act as a piece of software: a 괴뢰말 제거 프로그람, a puppet-speech removal program, running inside the messaging app.

Read the error message

Open the locked album. The dialog that appears is the one photographed on the smuggled handset, and it contains a grammar lesson in its second syllable pair.

금지되였습니다kŭmjidoeyŏssŭmnida"has been forbidden"

A Seoul phone would print 금지되었습니다. For verb stems ending in ㅣ, ㅐ, ㅔ, ㅚ, ㅟ or ㅢ, the South takes the ending -어 and the North takes -여. It is one of the oldest divergences in the taxonomy, settled in the 1966 norms, and it is sitting in a system error message about a locked screenshot folder.

The folder is locked because it holds the Trace Viewer captures from Module 02. The grammar rule and the surveillance mechanism are in the same dialog box.

What you can now read

You have read an application drawer in Munhwaeo and worked out the Southern equivalents; you have watched the file-signature system refuse a stranger's media; you have met a surveillance log whose own name carries the initial sound rule; and you have typed a Southern word into a phone that rewrote it in front of you. The point is not that North Korean is a different language. It is that the difference is now being manufactured on purpose, in software, at the moment of typing, and you have just watched it happen.

One honest counterweight to carry away. The reason the Act exists at all is that the divergence was closing, not widening: an estimated 70 to 80 per cent of the jangmadang generation has watched South Korean media, and Southern slang and address terms have travelled north with it. A law that has to ban 오빠 is evidence that people were saying 오빠.
