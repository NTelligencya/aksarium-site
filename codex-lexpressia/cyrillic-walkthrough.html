The Cyrillic alphabet

Where the great Slavic script came from, how an emperor and an academy trimmed it, and why the Russian keyboard keeps its commonest letters under your strongest fingers. Three modules, about ten minutes; wander through them in any order.

Module 01

The wrong man's alphabet

Myth we'll unpick: "Saint Cyril invented Cyrillic"

He did invent an alphabet for the Slavs. It just was not this one. The script that carries his name was built by other hands, in another country, after he was dead.

Two brothers and a brand-new script

In 863 the Byzantine emperor sent two brothers from Thessalonica, Constantine and Methodius, on a mission to Great Moravia. For it, Constantine devised Glagolitic, the first alphabet ever made for a Slavic language: some forty letters of loops, circles and crosses that look like no other script on earth. Only on his deathbed in Rome, in 869, did Constantine take the monastic name Cyril.

So the myth starts from something true: Cyril really was the first person to give the Slavs an alphabet. Hold that thought while the plot moves to Bulgaria.

Where the letters came from

So who did build Cyrillic?

Cyril died in 869. The script named after him appears in Bulgaria around the year 900. Who made it?

His students' students. The scholars of the Preslav literary school, heirs of the expelled Moravian mission, built the new script from the Greek uncial letters they wrote every day and kept Glagolitic-derived signs for the sounds Greek could not spell. They named it in honour of the teacher. Glagolitic did not die quietly: in Croatia it was still being carved and printed into the nineteenth century.

An alphabet named for a man who never wrote it. And it was about to meet an editor with absolute power.

Module 02

The tsar's red pen

Myth we'll unpick: "the alphabet the monks made is the one on screens today"

Between the monastery and your keyboard stand two great prunings: an emperor's, done with his own pen, and an academy's, finished by a revolution.

1708 · the civil script

Between 1708 and 1710 Peter the Great created the civil script (гражданский шрифт) for secular printing: letterforms redrawn to sit comfortably beside the Latin type of Western Europe, and several Greek-inherited letters struck out along with the thicket of accent marks above the line. Peter edited the proofs himself; a sheet with his own crossings-out survives. The first book off the press in the new type, in 1708, was a geometry textbook.

The Church kept the old letters, and Church Slavonic is still printed in them today. Among Peter's casualties for civil use: psi (ѱ) and omega (ѡ), Greek guests that Russian never needed.

Before Peter, numbers were letters

In manuscripts a wavy stroke called a titlo ran above the letters to mean "read me as a number"; here it is drawn as a plain line. Peter's reform swept the whole system away in favour of Arabic digits.

1918 · the academy's cut

The reform that finally deleted four letters was not a Bolshevik invention: it was drafted by the orthographic commission of the Imperial Academy of Sciences, first convened in 1904. The Provisional Government ordered the change in 1917, and the Soviet decree of October 1918 made it stick. Two words became one: миръ (peace) and міръ (world) collapsed into мир.

Tolstoy's title was always War and Peace (Война и миръ); the old spelling is exactly what lets you check the legend that it "really meant" War and the World.

Module 03

Thirty-three keys

Myth we'll unpick: "ЙЦУКЕН is just QWERTY wearing Cyrillic"

The Russian keyboard is not a translation of the American one. It is a different answer to the same problem, and in one respect a plainly better one.

Named by its own top row

Typewriters fitted for Russian went on sale in the late nineteenth century, and the layout that won the market is the direct ancestor of every Russian keyboard since. Like QWERTY, its name is simply the top row read left to right: Й Ц У К Е Н. Machines built before 1918 carried keys for ѣ and і; when the spelling reform killed those letters, the reform reached into the hardware and the dead keys were reassigned.

Thirty-three letters in three rows leaves little room for anything else: on the standard Russian layout the full stop and the comma share a single key, at the far right of the bottom row.

The letter in exile

The youngest letter on the board sits alone in the far top-left corner: ё. Princess Yekaterina Dashkova, head of two academies, proposed it at a session of the Russian Academy in 1783; the writer Nikolai Karamzin helped carry it into print. Two centuries on its dots are still optional in most Russian text, required mainly in dictionaries and children's books, and the keyboard shrugs it off to the corner key QWERTY uses for the tilde.

The dots do real work when they appear: все means "everyone" and всё means "everything". Most printed text simply trusts you to know which is meant.

Why this matters

An alphabet looks eternal and is anything but. Cyrillic was assembled by exiles from Greek capitals and missionary invention, pruned by an emperor's pen, trimmed by an academy, and finally sorted onto three rows of keys by the arithmetic of tired fingers. Every key on a Russian keyboard is a decision somebody made, and you can now read the reasons off the board.
